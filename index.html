Your brain is a prediction machine

Your brain doesn’t just wait for sounds to arrive. It’s always guessing what’s coming next. When you heard A, A, A in the game, your brain was already getting ready for another A before it played.

Scientists call this predictive coding. It works in three steps, over and over, many times a second:

  1. Guess

    Your brain uses what it has heard so far to predict the next sound.

  2. Check

    When the sound arrives, your brain compares it with its guess.

  3. Update

    If the guess was wrong, a “surprise” signal fires, and your brain changes its guess for next time.

That surprise signal is something scientists can measure. Have a listen:

Four Bs, then a C. Brain cells respond more strongly to the C, because it broke the pattern your brain had spotted.

Why bother predicting? It saves effort, because your brain only needs to pay close attention to the surprises. Predictions help you follow a conversation in a noisy room, catch a ball, and enjoy music, which is full of patterns that a good song sometimes breaks on purpose.

The harder levels hide the pattern inside groups, or link notes that aren’t next to each other. Your brain can learn these too. It’s a bit like how you learned the rules of your language just by listening, long before anyone explained grammar to you.

Who made this game

This game comes from the Cognitive and Translational Neuroscience Lab at Newcastle University, led by Professor Yuki Kikuchi.

We study how the brain hears, remembers and makes sense of sounds, including speech and animal calls. We want to understand the brain circuits that let us predict what we’ll hear next, and use what we learn to help develop treatments for conditions that affect the brain and mental health.

To do this, we listen in on the activity of brain cells, take pictures of the brain with MRI scanners, and run listening experiments a lot like this game.

Inside your brain

What are brain cells actually doing when you predict the next beep? Let’s look at their electrical activity, and the waves it makes.

Brain cells talk in tiny sparks

Your brain has about 86 billion brain cells, called neurons. They send messages to each other using tiny pulses of electricity called spikes. One neuron can fire many spikes every second.

Scientists think the brain uses these spikes to send two kinds of message, travelling in opposite directions:

Thinking areasmake guesses about what’s coming
↓ Guesses travel down↑ Surprises travel up
Hearing areascompare the guess with the real sound
↑ Sound comes in
Your ears

When a pattern is easy to predict, the guess matches what arrives, so there’s very little surprise to send up. When something unexpected happens, a burst of surprise signals travels up, and the thinking areas change their guess.

Brain waves

When thousands of neurons fire together in time, their activity rises and falls together, like a Mexican wave in a football stadium. No single fan is the wave, but together they make one you can see from across the ground.

These rhythms are called brain waves, or oscillations. They’re strong enough to pick up with sensors on the outside of your head, using a method called EEG. They come in different speeds, measured in hertz (Hz), which means waves per second. Here’s half a second of each:

How our lab studies this

We record brain waves and the spikes of individual neurons while listeners hear sound patterns like the ones in the game. We look for the guess signals and the surprise signals, and at how brain waves line up with what’s expected.

We also use a technique called closed-loop stimulation. It’s like the auto settings in the swing demo: a computer watches a brain wave as it happens and plays a sound at exactly the right moment in the wave. We’re testing whether this can nudge brain rhythms, and the memories and predictions that depend on them. One day, ideas like this might help people whose brain rhythms aren’t working as they should.

The waves in these demos are simplified cartoons. Real brain waves are much messier, which is part of what makes them so interesting to study.
